Appendix B — Mean Removal

B.1 Segmentation

Assume each time series has a piecewise-constant mean: \[X_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \mu_t \text{ piecewise constant.}\] Segmentation estimates \(\mu_t\) by the within-segment sample mean and subtracts it: \[\hat{\varepsilon}_t = X_t - \hat{\mu}_t.\] The residuals \(\hat{\varepsilon}_t\) are approximately stationary, so Pearson correlation applied to \((\hat{\varepsilon}_t^{(1)}, \hat{\varepsilon}_t^{(2)})\) estimates contemporaneous correlation between the two series.

B.2 Degrees of Freedom Correction

To do inference on Pearson correlation, we first estimate the correlation \(\hat{r}\). Then, we find that \[t_{\text{naive}} = \frac{r \sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}} \sim t_{n-2}.\] That is, the standard Pearson correlation has \(df = n-2\), because we use two degrees of freedom to estimate the mean and variance.

However, with segmentation, we must estimate multiple means. If there are \(K\) change points, then we must estimate \(K + 1\) sample means. In all, we must estimate \(K + 2\) parameters, so the correct statistic is \[t_{\text{correct}} = \frac{r\sqrt{n-K-2}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}} \sim t_{n-K-2}.\] Since both statistics share the same \(r\), they are related by \[t_{\text{naive}} = \lambda \cdot t_{\text{correct}}, \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{\frac{n-2}{n-K-2}} > 1.\]

The naive test rejects when \[|t_{\text{naive}}| > t_{n-2,\,\alpha/2}.\] Substituting \(t_{\text{naive}} = \lambda \cdot t_{\text{correct}}\), this is equivalent to \[|t_{\text{correct}}| > \frac{t_{n-2,\,\alpha/2}}{\lambda}.\] So the actual type I error of the naive test is \[P\!\left(|t_{\text{correct}}| > \frac{t_{n-2,\,\alpha/2}}{\lambda}\right), \qquad t_{\text{correct}} \sim t_{n-K-2}.\] Since \(\lambda > 1\), the threshold \(t_{n-2,\,\alpha/2}/\lambda < t_{n-K-2,\,\alpha/2}\), so the naive test rejects more often than it should.

Theoretical type I error. Given \(n\), \(K\), and \(\alpha\), the exact inflation is computable in closed form. Let \(c = t_{n-2,\,\alpha/2}\) be the naive critical value. The actual type I error is \[P\!\left(|T| > \frac{c}{\lambda}\right), \qquad T \sim t_{n-K-2}.\] In R: 2 * pt(-qt(1 - alpha/2, df = n-2) / lambda, df = n - K - 2).

Example. The table below uses \(\alpha = 0.05\) and \(K/n \approx 0.25\) (as in scenario(10, n)), showing that the inflation is determined by the ratio \(K/n\), not \(n\) alone.

\(n\) \(K\) \(\lambda\) Naive critical value Effective threshold Actual type I error
100 25 \(\sqrt{98/73} \approx 1.159\) \(t_{98,\,0.025} \approx 1.984\) \(1.984/1.159 \approx 1.712\) \(P(\lvert t_{73}\rvert > 1.712) \approx 0.091\)
500 125 \(\sqrt{498/373} \approx 1.155\) \(t_{498,\,0.025} \approx 1.965\) \(1.965/1.155 \approx 1.701\) \(P(\lvert t_{373}\rvert > 1.701) \approx 0.090\)
1000 249 \(\sqrt{998/749} \approx 1.155\) \(t_{998,\,0.025} \approx 1.962\) \(1.962/1.155 \approx 1.699\) \(P(\lvert t_{749}\rvert > 1.699) \approx 0.090\)

The simulation below confirms this. The histogram shows the corrected t-statistics alongside both candidate densities. When \(n\) and \(n - K\) are both large, the two \(t\)-curves are visually indistinguishable — the inflation does not appear in the bulk of the distribution, only in the rejection rate.
